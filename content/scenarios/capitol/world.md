# The Capital

You live and work in the capital of the United States, in the few streets between the White House and the Capitol. This is where the country is run: the President and his staff at the west end of the National Mall, the Senate under the great white dome at the east end. Everyone here knows everyone, or at least knows who matters, and a rumour crosses the Mall faster than a man can walk it.

**Era.** The present day. Phones, cars, television and computers exist out there, but none of that is part of your day here: you get things done face to face, by walking over, by talking, by handing someone a paper. Nobody inside these grounds carries a gun; the Secret Service carries batons. Money is in dollars: a cafeteria lunch costs ten dollars, a coffee two, a senator earns in a year what a factory worker earns in five.

**The place.** The National Mall is a long lawn with a stone walkway. At its west end stand the White House buildings: the Oval Office, the President's private residence, the White House Mess (the staff kitchen), the plain staff rooms in the basement, and a little further off the Vice President's house. At its east end stands the Capitol: the round Rotunda under the dome, the Senate chamber above it, the Senate cafeteria in the basement, and the senators' offices beside it. South of the Mall lies a long reflecting pool, and beyond it an old cemetery by the river. Behind the Capitol kitchens runs a service alley that respectable people never use.

**Politics.**
- Two parties: the Eagle Party (the President's party) and the Harvest Party (strong in farm country).
- The Senate passes laws by vote. If the vote is tied, the Vice President casts the deciding vote.
- The President signs orders and laws; without the Senate, a big tariff is not law.
- The press is everywhere. A sentence said in the wrong place is in tomorrow's headlines.
- Loyalty is the currency of the White House. Disloyalty is never forgiven.
- Taking money for a vote is a crime, and a career-ending scandal if it comes out.

**Hard times.** The economy is bad. Oil prices are the highest anyone can remember, and gas at the pump costs almost twice what it did two years ago. Farmers can't afford diesel, truckers are angry, and people blame Washington. The United Empire, the great trading power across the ocean, sells cars, steel and machines to America and buys its grain. The relationship has gone sour, and everyone has an opinion about it.
